In undergraduate statistics, we learned [moment generating functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moment-generating_function) and that it uniquely determines the distribution if exists. However moment generating function does not always exsits for all distributions. Characteristic functions always exists for all real-valued random variables and it provides alternative approach to working with distributions.

## Characteristic function
<div class="definition" text="characteristic function">
For a random variable $X$,
$$
\varphi_X(t) = Ee^{itX}
$$
is the characteristic function of $X$, where $i$ is the complex unit.
</div>

Or we could just notate it *ch.f.* for short. Every characteristic functions have the following properties regardless of the distribution.

<div class="prop" text="properties of ch.f.">
(i) $\varphi_X(0) = 1.$ <br>
(ii) $\varphi_X(-t) = \overline{\varphi_X(t)}.$ <br>
(iii) $|\varphi_X(t)| \le 1.$ <br>
(iv) $\varphi_X$ is uniformly continuous. <br>
(v) $\varphi_{aX+b} = e^{itb}\varphi_X(at).$ <br>
(vi) $X_1 \perp X_2$ then $\varphi_{X_1+X_2} = \varphi_{X_1}\varphi_{X_2}.$ <br>
</div>

<div class="proof"><br>
(iv)
$$
\begin{align}
|\varphi(t+h) - \varphi(t)|
  &= |E(e^{i(t+h)X - e^{itX}})| \\
  &\le E\cancelto{1}{|e^{itX}|}|e^{ihX - 1}|
\end{align}
$$
holds for all $t\in\mathbb{R}.$ By BCT, $E|e^{ihX}-1|\to0$ as $h\to\infty.$ Thus for all $\epsilon>0,$ there exists $h$ such that $|\varphi(t+h)-\varphi(t)|<\epsilon$ for all $t.$
</div>

other proofs are direct from the definition. Another property comes from the additivity of lebesgue integral.

<div class="lemma" text="3.3.9">
Let $F_1,\cdots,F_n$ be distributions that have $\varphi_1,\cdots,\varphi_n$ as ch.f.s respectively. Let $\lambda_i \ge 0,$ $\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_i = 1.$<br>
$\implies \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i F_i$ has a ch.f. $\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i \varphi_i.$
</div>

This simply says that the mixture of distributions have the ch.f. which is also the mixture.

<div class="proof"><br>
$$
\begin{align}
&\varphi_{\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_iF_i}
  = \int e^{itx} d\left(\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_iF_i(x)\right) \\
& = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i \int e^{itx} dF_n(x)
  = \sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_i\varphi_i.
\end{align}
$$
</div>

Using the lemma we can show still another property.

<div class="lemma">
$\varphi$ is a ch.f. $\implies \text{Re}\varphi$ and $|\varphi|^2$ are also ch.f.s.
</div>
<div class="proof"><br>
Let $X\sim F$ and $(-X)\sim F'$ be independent.<br>
(i) Let $Y$ be a random variable with distribution $F_Y(x) = 0.5F(x) + 0.5F'(x).$ By the lemma, $\varphi_Y = \text{Re}\varphi_X.$<br>
(ii) $\varphi_{X+X'} = \varphi_X\overline{\varphi_{X}} = |\varphi_X|^2.$
</div>

Here are some of the ch.f.s of well-known distributions.
exx.

<div class="example"><br>
(i) (Poisson) $X \sim \mathcal{P}(\lambda).$ $\varphi_X(t) = e^{\lambda (e^{it}-1)}.$<br>
(ii) (Normal) $X\sim \mathcal{N}(0,1).$ $\varphi_X(t) = e^{-t^2/2}.$<br>
(iii) (Exponential) $X\sim\text{Exp}(1).$ $\varphi_X(t) = \frac{1}{1-it}.$<br>
(iv) (Double exponential) $X\sim\text{DE}(1).$ $\varphi_X(t) = \frac{1}{1+t^2}.$
</div>

Note that the ch.f. of $\text{DE}(1)$ is a density of the Cauchy distribution.

For a random variable that has a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure, Characteristic function is in fact the [Fourier transform](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/FourierTransform.html) of the distribution function of $X.$ So we can naturally deduce that a proper inversion might fully recover the distribution from ch.f. <br><br>


## Inversion formula
<div class="theorem" text="inversion formula">
Let $\varphi(t) = \int e^{itx}d\mu(x)$ be the ch.f. of a probability measure $\mu.$ If $a<b,$ then
$$
\lim_T \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-T}^T \frac{e^{-ita}-e^{-itb}}{it} \varphi(t) dt = \mu(a,b) + \frac{1}{2}\mu\{a,b\}.
$$
</div>

Notice that it is not $\int_{-\infty}^\infty$ but $\lim_T \int_{-T}^T.$ We will find out why during the proof.

<details>
  <summary>
    expand proof
  </summary>
  
<div class="proof"><br>
Let $I_T$ be the integrand of the left hand side.
$$
\begin{align}
I_T &= \int_{-T}^T \int \frac{e^{-ita}-e^{-itb}}{it} e^{itx} d\mu(x) dt \\
    &= \int \int_{-T}^T \frac{1}{it}\left( e^{it(x-a)} - e^{it(x-b)} \right) dt d\mu(x) \\
    &= \int \int_{-T}^T \frac{1}{t} \left( \sin t(x-a) = \sin t(x-b) \right) dt d\mu(x) \\
    &= \int R(x-a, T) - R(x-b, T) d\mu(x)
\end{align}
$$
where $R(\theta,T) = \int_{-T}^T\frac{\sin \theta t}{t} dt.$ The first equality comes from Fubini's theorem since 
$$
\frac{e^{-ita}-e^{-itb}}{it} = \int_a^b e^{-itx}dx \le b-a
$$
so it is integrable. Now observe that if we let $S(T)=\int_0^T \frac{\sin x}{x}dx,$
$$
R(\theta,T)=\begin{cases}
\int_{-T\theta}^{T\theta} \frac{\sin x}{x} dx = 2\int_0^{T\theta} \frac{\sin x}{x}dx = 2S(T\theta) &,~ \theta\ge0 \\
-R(-\theta,T) = -2S(-T\theta) &,~ \theta < 0
\end{cases}
$$
We get $R(\theta,T) = 2\text{sgn}(\theta)S(T|\theta|).$ By the result of exercise 1.7.5 of the textbook, $S(T) \to \frac{\pi}{2}$ as $T\to\infty.$ Thus 
$$
R(x-a,T)-T(x-b,T) \to \begin{cases}
  2\pi &,~ a<x<b \\
  \pi  &,~ x=a \text{ or } x=b \\
  0    &,~ x<a \text{ or } x>b \\
\end{cases}
$$
Since $|R(\theta,T)| \le 2\sup_y\int_0^y\frac{\sin t}{t}dt < \infty,$ with the help of BCT we get
$$
\begin{align}
&\lim_T \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-T}^T \frac{e^{-ita}-e^{-itb}}{it} \varphi(t) dt \\
&= \lim_T \frac{1}{2\pi} \int R(x-a,T) - R(x-b,T) d\mu(x) \\
&= \frac{1}{2\pi} \int \lim_T \left( R(x-a,T)-R(x-b,T) \right) d\mu(x) \\
&= \frac{1}{2\pi} \left( 2\pi\mu(a,b) + \pi\mu\{a,b\} \right) \\
&= \mu(a,b) + \frac{1}{2}\mu\{a,b\}.
\end{align}
$$
</div></details>

By inversion formula, $\varphi_X$ is real if and only if $X$ is symmetric.

If we use $\int_{-\infty}^\infty$ instead then $\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{\sin \theta t}{t} dt$ would not be integrable. If we have $\int \|\varphi(t)\| dt < \infty,$ then it is possible to use $\int_{-\infty}^\infty.$ The next theorem is about this case.

<div class="theorem" text="inversion formula for density">
If $\int |\varphi(t)| dt < \infty,$ then $\mu$ has a bounded continuous density $f$ such that
$$
f(y) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-ity} \varphi(t) dt.
$$
</div>

Observe that it is exactly the same to the inverse Fourier transform. In fact, $\mu$ is *absolutely continuous* to the Lebesgue measure and $f=\frac{d\mu}{d\lambda}.$

<details>
  <summary>
    expand proof
  </summary>
  
<div class="proof">
From the proof the inversion formula, $|\frac{e^{-ita}-e^{-itb}}{it}| \le |b-a|.$ Since $\int |\varphi(t)| dt < \infty,$
$$
\frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{e^{-ita}-e^{-itb}}{it}\varphi(t)dt \le \frac{|b-a|}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^\infty |\varphi(t)|dt < \infty.
$$
Thus the inversion formula converges absolutely in this case and $\mu(a,b) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{e^{-ita}-e^{-itb}}{it}\varphi(t)dt.$ Letting $b \to a$ and it follows that $\mu$ has no point masses.<br>
&ensp; Using the above and Fubini's theorem we get
$$
\begin{align}
\mu(x,x+h)
  &= \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{e^{-itx}-e^{-it(x+h)}}{it} \varphi(t)dt \\
  &= \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^\infty \int_x^{x+h} e^{-ity} dy \varphi(t)dt \\
  &= \int_x^{x+h} \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-ity} \varphi(t) dt dy
\end{align}
$$
so $\frac{1}{2\pi} \int e^{-ity} \varphi(t) dt$ is a density of $\mu.$<br>
&ensp; $|f(y)| \le \frac{1}{2\pi} \int |\varphi(t)|dt < \infty$ and $f$ is bounded.<br>
&ensp; $|f(y+h) - f(y)| 
\le \frac{1}{2\pi} \int |e^{-ity}(e^{-ith}-1)\varphi(t)|dt 
\le \frac{1}{\pi} \int |\varphi(t)|dt < \infty.$ By DCT  $|f(y+h) - f(y)|\to0$ as $h\to0$ and $f$ is continuous.
</div>
</details>

The formula indicates that if the ch.f. is integrable, then the distribution has a continuous density and it can be directly calculated.<br><br>


## Continuity theorem

We now relate pointwise convergence of the characteristic function to weak convergence of distributions.

<div class="theorem" text="continuity theorem">
Let $\mu_n$, $1\le n\le \infty$ be probability measures with ch.f. $\varphi_n.$ Then<br>
(i) $\mu_n \overset{w}{\to} \mu_\infty$ $\implies \varphi_n \to \varphi_\infty.$ <br>
(ii) If there exists $\varphi$ such that $\varphi_n \to \varphi$ and $\varphi$ is continuous at $0,$ then $\mu_n \overset{w}{\to} \mu$ where $\mu$ is a probability measure with $\varphi$ as its ch.f.
