## Central limit theorem for i.i.d. sequences
We need three short lemmas on complex numbers for the proof of our first CLT.

<div class="lemma" text="3.4.3">
Let $\xi_1,\cdots,\xi_n,$ $\omega_1,\cdots,\omega_n \in \mathcal{C}$, $|xi_i|,|\omega_i| \le \theta.$ Then

$$\left|\prod_{i=1}^n \xi_i - \prod_{i=1}^n \omega_i\right| \le \theta^{n-1} \sum\limits_{i=1}^n |\xi_i - \omega_i|.$$
</div>

The proof is clear from induction.

<div class="lemma" text="3.4.4">
For $b \in \mathbb{C}$ such that $|b| \le 1,$ $|e^b - (1+b)| \le |b|^2.$
</div>
<div class="proof"><br>
Taylor expansion gives
$$e^b-(1+b) = \frac{b^2}{2!} + \frac{b^3}{3!} + \cdots.$$
Thus 
$$|e^b-(1+b)| \le \frac{|b|^2}{2}(1+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2^2}+\cdots)=|b|^2.$$
</div>

<div class="lemma" text="3.4.2">
For $c_n\to c\in \mathbb{ C},$ $(1+\frac{c_n}{n})^n \to e^c.$
</div>
<div class="proof"><br>
Let $\gamma > |c|$ then $\gamma > |c_n|$ for large $n$. By the two lemmas above,
$$\begin{aligned}
|e^{c_n} - (1+\frac{c_n}{n})^n| 
&\le |(e^{\frac{c_n}{n}})^n - (1+\frac{c_n}{n})^n| \\
&\le (e^{\frac{\gamma}{n}})^{n-1} |e^\frac{c_n}{n} - (1+\frac{c_n}{n})| \\
&\le e^\frac{(n-1)\gamma}{n} n |\frac{c_n}{n}|^2 \to 0
\end{aligned}$$
as $n\to\infty.$
</div>

Now we prove the main theorem.

<div class="theorem" text="CLT for iid sequence">
Let $X_1,\cdots,X_n$ be i.i.d. with $EX_1 = \mu$ and $\text{Var}(X_1)=\sigma^2 < \infty.$ $\implies \frac{S_n - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}} \overset{w}{\to} \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ as $n\to\infty.$
</div>

<details>
  <summary>
    expand proof
  </summary>
    
<div class="proof"><br>
Without loss of generality, let $\mu=0.$ Let $S=\frac{S_n}{\sigma\sqrt n}$ and $\varphi$ be ch.f. of $X.$ By <a href="https://naturale0.github.io/probability/PTE-3.3-characteristic-functions#continuity-theorem">continuity theorem</a>, showing $\varphi_S\to e^{-\frac{t^2}{2}}$ is enough. Since $EX^2 < \infty,$ <a href="https://naturale0.github.io/probability/PTE-3.3-characteristic-functions#estimation-of-the-error-term">the upper bound of the error</a> gives us the nice approximation $\varphi(t) = 1 - \frac{\sigma^2t^2}{2} + o(t^2).$

$$\varphi_S(t) = \left(\varphi\left(\frac{t}{\sigma\sqrt n}\right)\right)^n \\
= \left(1-\frac{t^2}{2n} + o\left(\frac{t^2}{\sigma^2n}\right)\right)^n \\
= \left(1 - \frac{t^2}{2n} + o\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)^n.$$
This with the lemmas gives us
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\left|\varphi_S(t) - \left(1-\frac{t^2}{2n}\right)^n\right| \\
&= \left| \left(\varphi\left(\frac{t}{\sigma\sqrt n}\right) \right)^n - \left(1-\frac{t^2}{2n}\right)^n \right| \\
&\le n \left| \varphi\left(\frac{t}{\sigma \sqrt n}\right) - \left( 1-\frac{t^2}{2n} \right) \right| \\
&= n \cdot o\left(\frac{1}{n}\right) \to 0
\end{aligned}
\]
as $n\to\infty$ and the proof is done.
</div>
</details>

The next example shows that unlike [the strong law of large numbers](https://naturale0.github.io/probability/PTE-2.4-strong-law-of-large-numbers#strong-law-of-large-numbers), pairwise independence is not enough for the central limit theorem.

<div class="example">
Let $\xi_1,\xi_2\cdots$ are i.i.d. with $P(\xi_1=z)=\frac{1}{2}$ if $z=1$ and $\frac{1}{2}$ if $z=-1.$ Define $X_i$'s as follows:
\[
\begin{aligned}
 & X_1 = \xi_1, \\
 & X_2 = X_1\xi_2, \\
 & X_3 = X_1\xi_3,~ X_4 = X_2\xi_3 \\
 & X_5 = X_1\xi_4,~ X_6 = X_2\xi_4,~ X_7 = X_4\xi_4 ,\cdots
\end{aligned}
\]
Then $X_i$'s are pairwise independent because $X_i$'s are products of different sets of $\xi_j$'s. Let 
\[
\begin{aligned}
S_{2^n} &= \sum\limits_{i=1}^{2^n} X_i \\
  &= \xi_1(1+\xi_2)\cdots(1+\xi_{n+1}) \\
  &=\begin{cases}
 \pm 2^n &,~ \text{with probability } \frac{1}{2^n} \\
 0         &,~ \text{with probability } 1-\frac{1}{2^n}
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
\]
so that $ES_{2^n}=0$ and $\text{Var}(S_{2^n}) = 2^{n-2}.$ Then
\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{S_{2^n}}{\sqrt{2^n}\sqrt{2^{n-2}}} 
 &= \frac{S_{2^n}}{s^{n-1}} \\
 &= \begin{cases}
  \pm 2^{\frac{n}{2}+1}   &,~ \text{with probability } \frac{1}{2^n} \\
  0   &,~ \text{with probability } 1-\frac{1}{2^n}
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
\]
and it does not converge weakly to $\mathcal{ N}(0,1).$
</div><br>

## Lindeberg-Feller condition
Our goal is to extend CLT for cases where identical distribution condition is not guaranteed. We again take advantage of triangular arrays for this.

<div class="theorem" text="Lindeberg-Feller">
$\{X_{nk}\}_{k=1}^{r_n}$ is a rowwise independent traingular array with $EX_{nk} = 0$ and $\text{Var}(X_{nk}) = \sigma_{nk}^2 < \infty.$ Let $s_n^2 = \sum_{k=1}^{r_n} \sigma_{nk}^2.$<br>
$\forall\epsilon>0,~ \lim_n \frac{1}{s_n^2} \sum\limits_{k=1}^{r_n} \int_{|X_{nk}|>\epsilon s_n} X_{nk}^2 dP = 0. \;\;\;\;(*)$<br>
 $\implies \frac{\sum_{k=1}^{r_n} X_{nk}}{s_n} \overset{w}{\to} \mathcal{ N}(0,1).$
</div>

We say $(\*)$ the *Lindeberg-Feller* or just *Lindeberg* condition. The condition basically says the tail part of $s_n^2$ is dominated by $s_n^2$ so that only $s_n^2 \approx \bar s_n^2$ where $\bar s_n^2 = \sum_{k=1}^n \int_{\|X_{nk}\|\le \epsilon s_n} X_{nk}^2 dP.$ Intuitively, like we truncated random variables for laws of large numbers, we truncate the variance of it.

<details>
  <summary>
    expand proof
  </summary>
  
<div class="proof"><br>
Let $S_n = X_{n1}+\cdots+X_{nr_n}$ be the rowwise sum and $\varphi_{nk}$ be ch.f. of $X_{nk}.$ Without loss of generality assume that $s_n^2 = 1.$ Then $\varphi_{S_n}(t) = \prod_{k=1}^{r_n}\varphi_{nk}(t).$ so
\[
\begin{aligned}
&|\varphi_{S_n}(t) - e^{-\frac{t^2}{2}}| \\
  &= \left| \prod_{k=1}^{r_n} \varphi_{nk}(t) - \prod_{k=1}^{r_n} e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{nk}^2 t^2} \right| \\
  &\le \sum_{k=1}^{r_n} |\varphi_{nk}(t) - e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{nk}^2 t^2}| \\
  &\le \sum_{k=1}^{r_n} \left\{ 
   \underbrace{\left|\varphi_{nk}(t) - (1-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{nk}^2t^2)\right|}_\text{(i)} 
   + \underbrace{\left|(1-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{nk}^2t^2) - e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{nk}^2 t^2}\right|}_\text{(ii)} 
  \right\}
\end{aligned}
\]<br> 
\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{(i)} &\le E\left( \frac{|tX_{nk}|^3}{3!} \wedge \frac{2|tX_{nk}|^2}{2!} \right) \\
&\le E\left( |tX_{nk}|^3 \wedge |tX_{nk}|^2 \right) \\
&= \int_{|X_{nk}|\le\epsilon} |tX_{nk}|^3 \wedge |tX_{nk}|^2 dP + \int_{|X_{nk}|>\epsilon} |tX_{nk}|^3 \wedge |tX_{nk}|^2 dP \\
&\le \int_{|X_{nk}|\le\epsilon} |tX_{nk}|^3 dP + t^2 \int_{|X_{nk}|>\epsilon} X_{nk}^2 dP \\
&\le \epsilon |t|^3 s_n^2 + t^2 \int_{|X_{nk}|>\epsilon} X_{nk}^2 dP
\end{aligned} 
\]
By the Lindeberg condition, right-most term goes to 0 as $n\to\infty.$ Since was $\epsilon>0$ was arbitrary, $\text{(i)}\to0.$<br>

&ensp; Now observe that for small $|t|,$ $|-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{nk}^2 t^2| < 1$ so by the lemma 
\[
|e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{nk}^2 t^2} - (1-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{nk}^2 t^2)| \le |-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{nk}^2 t^2| = \frac{1}{4}\sigma_{nk}^4 t^4
\]
and it follows:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{(ii)} 
&\le \sum\limits_{k=1}^{r_n} \frac{1}{4} \sigma_{nk}^4 t^4 \\
&\le \frac{1}{4} t^4 \sum\limits_{k=1}^{r_n} \max_{1\le k\le r_n} \sigma_{nk}^2 \cdot \sigma_{nk}^2 \\
&= \frac{1}{4} t^4 \max_{1\le k\le r_n} \sigma_{nk}^2.
\end{aligned}
\]
Note that
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_{nk}^2 
 &= \int_{|X_{nk}|\le\epsilon} X_{nk}^2 dP + \int_{|X_{nk}|>\epsilon} X_{nk}^2 dP \\
 &\le \epsilon^2 + \sum\limits_{k=1}^{r_n} \int_{|X_{nk}|>\epsilon} X_{nk}^2 dP
\end{aligned}
\]
and the last term is independent to $k.$ By the Lindeberg condition, $\max_{1\le k\le r_n}\sigma_{nk}^2 \to 0$ and $\text{(ii)} \to 0.$<br>
&ensp; $\therefore \varphi_{S_n}(t) \to e^{-\frac{t^2}{2}}.$
</div></details>

Before moving on to Lyapounov condition, I would like to remark two more points. First, as we can see in the proof, if the Lindeberg condition is met 
\\[
\max_{1\le k\le r_n} \frac{\sigma_{nk}^2}{s_n} 
= \max_{1\le k\le r_n} \frac{\sigma_{nk}^2}{\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{r_n}\sigma_{nk}^2}} \to 0.
\\]
Second, the Lindeberg condition always holds for i.i.d. sequences. Suppose $X_1,X_2,\cdots \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}(0,\sigma^2)$ then 
\\[
\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\int_{|X_k|>\epsilon\sigma\sqrt{n}} X_k^2 dP \to 0,~ \forall \epsilon > 0
\\]
by DCT. In such manner, we can think of it as a generalization of the CLT for i.i.d. sequences.<br><br>


## Lyapounov condition
Sometimes it is easier to show sufficiency of the Lindeberg condition than to directly show the condition. Lyapounov condition is the one.

<div class="definition" text="Lyapounov condition">
Let $\{X_{nk}\}_{k=1}^{r_n}$ be a rowwise independent triangular array. $\{X_{nk}\}_{k=1}^{r_n}$ satisfies the Lyapounov condition if for some $\delta>0,$<br>
(i) $E|X_{nk}^{2+\delta}| < \infty.$ <br>
(ii) $\frac{1}{s_n^{2+\delta}} \sum\limits_{k=1}^{r_n} \int |X_{nk}|^{2+\delta} dP \to 0.$
</div>

<div class="theorem" text="Lyapounov">
If $\{X_{nk}\}_{k=1}^{r_n}$ satisfies Lyapounov condition, then it satisfies Lindeberg condition as well.
</div>
<div class="proof"><br>
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{1}{s_n^2} \sum\limits_{k=1}^{r_n} \int_{|X_{nk}|>\epsilon s_n} X_{nk}^2 dP \\
&= \frac{1}{s_n^2} \sum\limits_{k=1}^{r_n} \int_{\frac{|X_{nk}|}{\epsilon s_n}>1} X_{nk}^2 dP \\
&\le \frac{1}{s_n^2} \sum\limits_{k=1}^{r_n} \int_{\frac{|X_{nk}|}{\epsilon s_n}>1} \left( \frac{|X_{nk}|}{\epsilon s_n} \right)^\delta \cdot X_{nk}^2 dP \\
&\le \frac{1}{\epsilon^\delta} \frac{1}{s_n^{2+\delta}} \sum\limits_{k=1}^{r_n} \int |X_{nk}|^{2+\delta} dP \to 0.
\end{aligned}
\]
</div><br>

### Lindeberg and Lyapounov conditions of higher order
In fact, the condition introduced above is sometimes phrased *Lyapounov condition of order $r=2+\delta.$* Corresponding Lindeberg condition of order $r>2$ is as follows.

<div class="definition" text="Lindeberge condition of order r">
\[
\forall \epsilon>0,~ \frac{1}{s_n^r}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{r_n} \int_{|X_{nk}|>\epsilon s_n} |X_{nk}|^r dP \to 0 \text{ as } n \to \infty.
\]
</div>

It can be shown that both conditions are equivalent in order $r>2.$<br><br>


## Examples
As an example, we continue to prove the unproven part of the [three series theorem](https://naturale0.github.io/probability/PTE-2.5-convergence-of-random-series#kolmogorovs-three-series-theorem).

<div class="proof" text="the three series theorem">
($\Rightarrow$)<br>
(i) Suppose $\sum_n P(|X_n|>A)=\infty.$ Then by the second Borel-Cantelli lemma, $P(|X_n|>A \text{ i.o.}) = 1$ and $\sum_{n=1}^\infty X_n$ does not converge ($\rightarrow\!\leftarrow$).<br>
(ii) Suppose $\sum_n \text{Var}(Y_n) = \infty.$ Let $c_n = sum_{k=1}^n \text{Var}(Y_k),$ $u_{nk} = \frac{Y_k - EY_k}{\sqrt c_n},$ then $Eu_{nk} = 0$ and $s_n^2 = \sum_{k=1}^n \text{Var}(u_{nk}) = 1.$ Since for all $\epsilon>0,$ $|u_{nk}|\le \frac{2A}{\sqrt c_n} < \epsilon$ for large $n,$ $\int_{|u_{nk}|>\epsilon} u_{nk}^2 dP = 0$ and Lindeberg condition is met. Hence $\sum_{k=1}^n u_{nk} \overset{w}{\to} \mathcal{N}(0,1). \;(*)$ From this result, we get
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\sum_n P(|X_n|>A) < \infty \\
&\implies P(X_n=Y_n \text{ eventually}) = 1 \\
&\implies \sum_n X_n \text{ converges iff } \sum_n Y_n \text{ converges}
\end{aligned} 
\]
By (i), $\sum_n Y_n$ converges and $\frac{\sum_{k=1}^n Y_k}{\sqrt c_n}\to0 \text{ a.s.} \;(**)$<br>
&ensp; By $(*),(**)$ and Slutsky's theorem, $-\frac{1}{\sqrt c_n}\sum_{k=1}^n EY_k \overset{w}{\to} \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ which is the contradiction since a sequence of real numbers cannot converge to a distribution.<br>
(iii) By (ii), $\sum_n Y_n$ converges a.s. $\sum_n (Y_n - EY_n)$ also converges by <a href="https://naturale0.github.io/probability/PTE-2.5-convergence-of-random-series#kolmogorovs-three-series-theorem">theorem 2.5.6</a>. Thus $\sum_n EY_n$ converges.
</div>
